# First-Time Cat Owner Guide: Feeding, Water & Health
## Research Cluster 2 (Indoor, 5kg Adult Black Cat, Malta)

## 1. FEEDING A 5KG INDOOR ADULT CAT

**Daily Calorie Requirement:**
A neutered indoor cat weighing 5kg requires approximately **250–300 kcal/day**. Use the baseline of 25–35 calories per pound body weight, adjusted downward for neutered cats (lower activity) and for indoor-only status. Individual variation is significant—scale portions based on body condition score (see below) and weigh food with a kitchen scale, never estimate by eye.

**Wet vs. Dry vs. Mixed Strategy (Evidence-Based):**

Cornell Feline Health Center and veterinary consensus strongly favor **mixed feeding (wet + dry)** for indoor adult cats:
- **Wet food (primary meal)**: Contains 75%+ moisture, providing critical hydration that cats chronically under-drink. Cats are desert-origin animals with weak thirst drive; wet food is the single most important intervention for FLUTD and urinary crystals prevention. Calorie density is lower (~80–100 kcal per 85g pouch), aiding weight management. Highly palatable, reducing "picky eater" friction.
- **Dry food (secondary, small portion)**: Used in moderation for dental benefit (minimal—brushing/VOHC treats far more effective), convenience, and cost. Contains only 6–10% moisture. **Free-feeding dry is the #1 cause of obesity in indoor cats** because cats cannot self-regulate calorie intake on dry food; the appetite signal defaults to "always eating."

**Recommended Feeding Schedule for 5kg indoor cat:**
- Morning: 1–2 pouches wet food (170–340 kcal, depending on brand)
- Small evening meal: 15–25g dry food + optional 1 wet pouch, OR dry food only
- **Total: 250–300 kcal/day** (adjust up/down per body score)

**Free-Feeding Traps:**
- Dry kibble left out ad libitum → obesity epidemic (cat eats out of boredom, not hunger)
- Wet food left out >2 hours → spoilage and bacterial growth
- **Solution:** Portion-controlled, scheduled meals only. No bowl grazing.

**Body Condition Score (BCS):**
Ideal BCS = 5/9. You should feel ribs easily with light pressure but not see them. Waist should be visible from above. Cats at BCS 7–9 face diabetes, FLUTD, arthritis, and shortened lifespan. Schedule vet weigh-ins every 3 months; adjust portions if drifting.

**Food Transitions:**
When switching foods (new brand, wet-to-dry ratio change), use the **7–10 day gradual transition**:
- Days 1–3: 75% old + 25% new
- Days 4–6: 50% old + 50% new
- Days 7–10: 25% old + 75% new
- Day 10+: 100% new

**Critical Risk—Hepatic Lipidosis (Fatty Liver Syndrome):**
Cats are uniquely vulnerable. Unlike dogs/humans, they cannot mobilize fat safely during starvation. **If a cat stops eating for >2–3 days, hepatic lipidosis can develop**, triggering jaundice, organ failure, and 6–7 weeks of tube feeding for recovery. This is the reason **"he'll eat when hungry" is dangerous advice**—cats will literally starve rather than accept food they dislike. If your cat refuses new food after 3–5 days, return to the old food and consult your vet before retrying. Never force a diet change that results in anorexia.

**Food Puzzle & Slow Feeder Value:**
- **Why it matters:** Cats are hunters; mealtimes mimic natural predation, satisfying instinct and slowing consumption (prevents regurgitation, improves digestion).
- **Practical effect:** Extends meal duration from 5 min → 15–20 min, increasing satiety and reducing obesity risk.
- **Does not help weight loss alone** but is a useful tool within a calorie-controlled regimen.

## 2. WATER & HYDRATION (CRITICAL FOR URINARY HEALTH)

**The Dehydration Problem:**
Cats evolved in arid environments with low thirst drive. Domestic cats drink 50–60% less than dogs, often getting 10–15% of daily water intake from food. On dry food only, this deficit compounds: urine becomes concentrated → mineral supersaturation → crystal formation → FLUTD, blockage, kidney disease.

**FLUTD & Urinary Crystals (Especially Male Cats):**
- **Males are 3× more likely to experience blockage** because the urethra is narrower and longer.
- Dehydration is the #1 modifiable risk factor. Wet food + fountain + proper hydration prevent ~70% of FLUTD cases.
- Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease is a syndrome (not a single disease): bladder inflammation, crystal formation (struvite or calcium oxalate), urethral spasms, mucus plugs. Combined, they obstruct urine flow.
- **Blockage is a medical emergency**—a male cat unable to urinate can develop kidney failure, hyperkalemia, and cardiac arrhythmia within 24–48 hours.

**Water Intake Optimization:**

1. **Fountain vs. Bowl (Evidence):**
   - Controlled studies in healthy cats show **no consistent population-level increase** in average water intake from fountains vs. static bowls.
   - **However**, individual cats show strong preferences: many cats drink more from a fountain because moving water signals freshness/safety (instinctive behavior).
   - **Practical recommendation:** Offer a fountain *and* a bowl. If your cat uses the fountain, keep it running daily.

2. **Whisker Fatigue & Bowl Design:**
   - Cats dislike whiskers touching bowl edges (sensory overload). Deep, narrow bowls are especially disliked.
   - **Solution:** Use wide, shallow bowls, or dedicated fountain with low-profile water surface.

3. **Water Placement:**
   - Keep water away from food (cats prefer to drink far from eating areas in the wild).
   - Multiple water stations throughout the home increase access.

4. **Wet Food as Hydration:**
   - 2–3 pouches wet food (~170–340g) = ~200–250ml of water daily → essential for hydration.
   - This is the single most effective way to increase feline water intake.

## 4. PREVENTIVE HEALTH FOR INDOOR CAT IN MALTA

**Core Vaccinations (Indoor Adult):**
- **FVRCP (Feline Viral Rhinotracheitis, Calicivirus, Panleukopenia):** Primary series (3 doses at 8, 12, 16 weeks for kittens); boosters every **3 years** for indoor adults. This is the core vaccine.
- **Rabies:** Legally required in Malta (21-day minimum post-vaccination before travel). One dose; then every 1–3 years depending on vaccine type and vet recommendation. **Microchip must be implanted BEFORE rabies vaccination.**
- **FeLV (Feline Leukemia Virus):** Recommended if cat goes outdoors; optional for indoor-only cats at very low risk. Primary series (2 doses, 2–4 weeks apart); booster at 1 year, then every 1–3 years.

**Malta-Specific Parasite Prevention:**
- **Fleas:** Despite indoor status, fleas can enter homes on clothing, other pets, or open windows/doors. Monthly topical (Revolution Plus) or oral (Comfortis, Capstar) recommended year-round in Mediterranean climate.
- **Heartworm:** Present in southern Europe/Mediterranean. Indoor cats at lower risk but some exposure possible. **Consult your Maltese vet on endemic risk**; if recommended, monthly heartworm preventive (e.g., Revolution Plus contains heartworm coverage).
- **Leishmania:** Endemic in Mediterranean basin (Malta included). Vaccine exists (CaniLeish for dogs; feline vaccine not widely available/recommended). Discuss risk with vet; focus on flea prevention (transmitted by sandflies) and avoiding outdoor exposure at dawn/dusk.
- **Intestinal Worms:** Monthly all-in-one preventives (e.g., Revolution Plus) cover roundworms, hookworms. Tapeworms less common indoors but possible; discuss annual screening with vet.

**Microchipping:**
- **Legally required in Malta** for pets over 12 weeks of age (per EU pet travel/import regulations).
- Non-encrypted, 15-digit ISO 11784/11785 compliant chip.
- Must be implanted BEFORE rabies vaccination (regulatory requirement).
- Cost: €20–50 (typical Malta clinic fee).
- Register chip with your contact details in the national database.

**Annual Vet Check:**
- Weight, BCS assessment, dental exam, urinalysis (especially for males).
- Blood work baseline at age 7–10 years or sooner if health issues emerge.

**Pet Insurance in Malta:**
Several providers offer feline coverage:
- **Laferla:** Essential & Premier tiers; reputedly low paperwork
- **MAPFRE Middlesea:** €1,000 max per policy period (capped, not unlimited)
- **MelitaUnipol (via MUIA):** Accident + illness + optional routine care
- **helpAcat.mt:** Dedicated feline insurer

**Ballpark cost:** €15–45/month depending on age & coverage level. Most require 3+ month waiting period for pre-existing conditions. **Action:** Obtain quotes from 2–3 providers; check excess/co-pay and annual/lifetime limits before enrolling.

**Dental Care (Realistic Approach):**
- **Gold standard:** Daily toothbrushing with VOHC-approved feline toothpaste (HealthyMouth, Oxyfresh). Few cats tolerate it; worth trying early for habituation.
- **Practical alternative:** VOHC-approved dental treats (Greenies Feline, Purina DentaLife) + water additive (HealthyMouth or Oxyfresh water additive). Studies show modest plaque reduction; better than nothing.
- **Professional cleaning:** Every 1–3 years if tartar accumulates. Not routine for indoor cats with good diet + preventives; discuss with vet based on exam findings.
- **Avoid:** Dry kibble as a dental cleaner (minimal effect; high-carb diet worse for teeth than benefit of mechanical action).

**Hairball Management (Black Cat Grooming):**
- **Brush 3–4× per week minimum** (daily for long-haired cats); black coat shows matting less visibly but it's still there.
- **Tools:** Start with slicker brush (Safari, Hertzko, or FURminator) → follow with metal comb (Frisco, Andis) to detect/remove remaining tangles.
- **Hairball diet:** Choose formula with added fiber (e.g., Purina Pro Plan Hairball Control) or supplement with pumpkin (1 tsp/day mixed in food).
- **Hydration:** Wet food + fountain prevent hairballs by improving gut motility and urine dilution.
- **Laxatives (rare):** Only if cat is vomiting hairballs repeatedly; discuss with vet (Laxatone, Paraffin).

## 5. THE 3-3-3 RULE FOR ADOPTED ADULT CATS

Your 2-year-old black cat will follow this timeline (not a guarantee, but a realistic guide):

| Phase | Days | What to Expect | Your Role |
|-------|------|----------------|-----------|
| **Decompression** | 0–3 days | Hiding, frozen, scared. Eats/drinks little. May not use litter box. Overwhelming stimulus overload. | Quiet room (bedroom or spare bath). Litter box + food + water in room. Minimal handling. Let cat hide. |
| **Settling In** | 3–21 days | Emerges gradually. Learns routine (feeding times, your schedule, house sounds). Becomes curious. May still be skittish. Bonding begins slowly. | Consistent schedule. Play with toys (no forced interaction). Reward approach with treats. Let cat set pace. |
| **At Home** | 21–90 days | Truly at home. Shows personality, preferences, regular affection. Comfortable on furniture/bed. Plays, grooms, confident. | Full integration. Normal routines. Expect adjustment of behavior over time. |

**Patience is more important than timeline.** A cat with prior trauma may take 6+ months. Consistency (same feeding time, play routine, quiet environment) accelerates trust more than rushing.

## RED-FLAG EMERGENCY SYMPTOMS — WHEN TO CALL VET IMMEDIATELY

| Symptom | Why Critical | Action |
|---------|--------------|--------|
| **Male cat straining in litter box with no/minimal urine output** | Urinary blockage → kidney failure, cardiac arrhythmia, death in 24–48h | **Call vet NOW.** Do not wait. This is the #1 male cat emergency. |
| **Cat not eating for >24–48 hours** | Risk of hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver) which develops in 2–7 days of anorexia | Call vet same day. Do not assume "he'll eat when hungry." |
| **Vomiting 2+ times in 24 hours or vomiting + lethargy** | Could be GI obstruction (string, hairball), toxin, or acute illness | Call vet same day. Bring sample of vomit if possible. |
| **Crying in pain, panting, restlessness, hiding + lethargy** | Acute pain (often FLUTD, blockage, trauma, or peritonitis) | Call vet NOW. |
| **Visible string/ribbon hanging from mouth or anus** | Linear foreign body → intestinal bunching/perforation | Call vet NOW. Do not pull string out (can perforate bowel). |
| **Pale gums, jaundice (yellow tinge to gums/eyes), dark urine** | Anemia, liver failure, or hemolysis (onion/garlic/acetaminophen toxicity) | Call vet NOW. |
| **Difficulty breathing, open-mouth breathing, drooling** | Upper airway obstruction, severe respiratory distress, or toxin reaction | Call vet NOW. |
| **Facial/paw swelling, drooling excessively, difficulty swallowing** | Anaphylaxis, toxin (paracetamol), or foreign body lodged in mouth | Call vet NOW. |
